Step 1 of 5

Assess

Before you analyze data or run a test, agree on the question. Define the business goal, choose KPIs that measure it, check that your data sources can record them, and know what each measurement method can and cannot tell you.

SMART goals

The best-defined business goals are specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound. A vague goal makes every later decision harder, because you can no longer tell whether your analysis or test measured the right thing. Axion, a TV maker with great technology but low awareness, drafted the two goals below. Mark the criteria each one meets, then check.

Study guide p. 5
From goal to KPI

A KPI makes explicit which outcome you will judge success by. KPIs are more than metrics: they tell a story about the business at the time you measure. To find yours, ask what success looks like, and whether your current metrics are proxies or truly track business outcomes.

Pair each goal with its KPI: select a goal, then its KPI.

Study guide p. 6
Primary and secondary KPIs

Primary KPI

The overall business objective that ad spend is measured against; often the main question the business wants answered. Example: ad recall lift.

Secondary KPI

Objectives that add context to the analysis and can also be measured against ad spend. Example: ad recall lift for a specific audience reached with video or Reels.

For Axion’s awareness goal, the primary KPI is the percentage increase in brand awareness among home theater enthusiasts; a secondary KPI could be website visits in Q1.

Know the limits of a KPI

  • Measurable: some objectives can’t be tracked with the tools you have, such as purchases paid in cash.
  • Primary versus secondary: focus on the primary KPI and don’t switch KPIs mid-campaign.
  • Accurate measurement: if there isn’t enough data for a metric, switch to another KPI or a proxy metric.

Misalignment misleads: Axion wanted to sell 10% more TVs year over year but its digital team tracked clicks. It should have measured conversions.

Study guide p. 6
KPIs versus metrics

Metrics make a KPI measurable with data. A metric can be a number or a ratio, and you can estimate it or observe it. All KPIs are metrics, but not all metrics are KPIs.

Choose metrics that reflect business results. Likes, shares and comments can be useful, but people who engage with a paid ad are not necessarily more likely to buy, and people who never engage can still be influenced. An ad with low engagement is not automatically ineffective.

Storx, an online baby store, wanted 100,000 people on its TeleNanny page by the end of Q2. The KPI was the number of page visitors, and the metric used to track it was click-through rate.

Study guide p. 8
Proxy metrics

A proxy metric stands in for an outcome you can’t measure directly. Notablé sells chocolates through grocery stores it doesn’t own, so it has no sales data. To steer toward a 10% sales goal, it tracks reach and frequency instead, assuming more exposure helps shoppers remember the brand in store.

Proxies such as clicks and likes are indirect and may not correlate with business value, so treat them with care.

Study guide pp. 29, 78
Inventory your data sources

A data source is a tool, connection or piece of code that collects information you can later measure and analyze. Ask which sources suit your success metrics, and which ones are actually in place.

First-party data

Collected by the business directly from its customers: website activity, sales data.

Third-party data

Collected by a party with no direct relationship to the customer, such as a data management provider.

Types of data you can analyze

  • Actions people take in your stores, website and app, including messaging
  • CRM data, available as first- or third-party data
  • Campaign performance, as in Ads Manager
  • Test results, such as the incremental impact of a campaign
  • Overall marketing performance, as in a marketing mix model

Study guide pp. 10–11
Use several sources together

For the best results, use the Conversions API with the Pixel. The Conversions API is a server-to-server connection, so it can share website events the Pixel loses to connectivity problems or loading errors, and it can also share offline events or events that happen later.

To find gaps, compare a small sample from the order system with the events sent through the Conversions API.

Event Match Quality (EMQ) shows how well the customer information you send can be matched to accounts. Advertisers with higher EMQ tend to get better conversion lift outcomes.

Study guide p. 11
Variables and granularity

A variable is a factor likely to change. The independent variable is the one you control (budget, optimization, objective); the dependent variable is the outcome that responds (sales, cost per result). Choose variables that are specific, measurable and tied to the goal.

Granularity is the level of detail. Reconcile it before you analyze:

  • Time: one source reports by week, another by day.
  • Geography: ZIP or post codes versus cities or market areas.
  • Definitions: one tool counts a video view after 2 seconds, another from the start.
Study guide p. 11
Four ways to handle outliers
  1. Analyze with and without them. With a large sample, dropping one may not matter. If the outcome changes, use a median analysis.
  2. Down-weight them with a weighted average, so they influence the result less.
  3. Adjust the sample. A trimmed mean drops a small share of the highest and lowest values; a Winsorized mean replaces the extremes with their nearest neighbors.
  4. Remove them, with caution. Outliers can tell a story, or reveal a factor you didn’t know about. Investigate before you delete.

Step 2 of 5

Hypothesize

A test is only as useful as the question it answers. Build a hypothesis that names who, what, where, when and why, turn it into a null and an alternative you can test, then choose the measurement approach that can prove or disprove it.

What makes a hypothesis strong

A hypothesis is an idea you can test. It should relate to the primary business goal, and the more data and insight behind it, the stronger it gets. A strong hypothesis answers five questions:

  • Who: the audience
  • What: the behavior of that audience
  • Where: the location
  • When: the ad schedule
  • Why: the rationale for the behavior you expect

A basic hypothesis covers who and what. A better one adds where and when. A strong one adds why.

Variables you can test

Audience and placements, ad creative (format, message, number of ads), delivery optimization, campaign objective, account structure, budget and frequency.

Study guide pp. 17–18; i-DAC tutorial, test variables
From hypothesis to test hypothesis

A test hypothesis is a proposed explanation you can test, usually written as if/then. Where a hypothesis can be a subjective assumption, a test hypothesis includes a null and an alternative. The aim is to see whether you can reject the null and accept the alternative.

  1. Identify the independent variable (what you change) and the dependent variable (the outcome).
  2. Make both specific, measurable and aligned with the business goal.
  3. Test one variable at a time if you want to isolate its effect.
  4. State the null and the alternative so it’s clear what you’re testing.
Study guide p. 18
Worked example: placements and cost

If an advertiser increases the number of ad placements from 1 to 4 or more, their average cost per result will decrease.

  • Independent variable: number of ad placements
  • Dependent variable: cost per result
H0: CPR1=CPR4+ H1: CPR4+<CPR1
Watch the direction. The alternative has to match the “then” clause: more placements, lower cost. The study guide prints this inequality the other way round, so read the if/then statement first.

Five ways to prove or disprove a hypothesis
Cross-channel reach reporting observational

Measures how channels such as email, TV, direct mail, Facebook and paid search work together to generate outcomes.

Limitations: metrics vary by channel; not all channels share touchpoint or reach data; like-for-like data may be missing; reach doesn’t always correlate with brand or conversion outcomes.

Example: a CPG advertiser on TV and digital wants broad reach at an efficient cost per person, and tests whether TV delivers more efficient reach than its digital channels.

Attribution observational

Determines how much credit each touchpoint on the path to conversion gets. Multi-touch attribution (MTA) credits more than one touchpoint; data-driven attribution assigns credit with historical data and statistical modeling.

Limitations: statistical models can be costly or limited to certain channels; cross-device gaps in cookie- or web-based systems; many tools don’t see the full path (in-app impressions are often not shared between publishers); often digital-only.

Example: during its peak sales period an advertiser won’t hold out media, but wants to know whether prospecting delivers less incremental value than re-engagement. It uses data-driven attribution as an interim estimate.

Marketing mix modeling observational

A statistical analysis that quantifies the incremental sales impact and ROI of marketing and non-marketing activities, offline and online, across channels.

Limitations: can miss small incremental increases; needs modelers and an econometric model; doesn’t help in-channel optimization; shows correlation, not causation; time-intensive.

Example: an agency believes TV earns a higher ROI than direct mail for a soda brand, so TV should get more budget next year. An MMM shows each channel’s effect on last year’s sales.

A/B test test

Runs campaigns against each other to see which approach produces the best results on your KPIs.

Limitations: some A/B tests have only randomized test groups and no control, so they don’t measure causality or incremental value; avoid them when the strategies have different baseline conversion rates (such as different audiences); groups may lack participation.

Example: an advertiser running only in Facebook Feed tests whether adding Instagram Feed lowers its average cost per result.

Randomized controlled trial test

Designed to measure causality. Participants are randomized into mutually exclusive groups; the test group gets the treatment and the control group does not, giving a standard of comparison.

Limitations: may lack power (budget too low, too short, too few conversions); power is hard to calculate without history; the control can be contaminated by media outside the test; the treatment may not be isolated; may miss the full purchase cycle or offline impact; outliers and variance handled unevenly.

Example: an advertiser on several channels hypothesizes that its Facebook ads drive $500,000 in incremental sales at $50 per incremental purchase in June, and checks its attribution model with an RCT.

Study guide pp. 20–24
After the verdict

Once you’ve validated or invalidated the hypothesis, decide what each outcome means and recommend next steps. A validated hypothesis usually leads to a new one: insights raise new questions, and those become the next tests. Keep thinking beyond what you’ve already tested.

Study guide p. 23
Read the evidence

The p-value is the probability of results at least as extreme as the ones you observed, assuming the null hypothesis is true. You choose the threshold α before you look.

reject H0 if p<α

R², the coefficient of determination, is the share of variance in the dependent variable that the independent variables predict. Use it to compare how well models fit.

Study guide p. 25
Build the narrative

Explain why the hypothesis held or failed: put the data in context, state the caveats and acknowledge that the customer path is complex. Then ask whether the approach really measured the KPI. Watch for:

  • Dilution during a test
  • Offline transactions left out of a conversion lift test
  • Insufficient statistical power
  • Contamination between test and control cells

Retest when results were contaminated or inconclusive, or when you want to validate them. Example: a test failed to reject the null, then the team found a Pixel problem that undercounted conversions. Fix the data flow first, then retest.

Step 3 of 5

Recommend measurement solutions

Pick the solution that can answer your hypothesis, design it so it can detect the effect, and know what each one reports. Lift tests prove causality, A/B tests pick winners fast, and attribution and marketing mix models fill in where you can’t hold out an audience.

Ready to design a test?

You can design a test once you have all four of these:

  1. A business goal
  2. A primary KPI
  3. A strong hypothesis
  4. A variable to test

Keep tests as simple as possible: every extra cell adds complexity. You can also chain tests, for example an A/B test to choose between two creatives, then a conversion lift test to see how many incremental conversions the winner drives.

Single-cell, multi-cell and nested tests

Single-cell

One test group and one control group. Answers: how many incremental conversions (or how much brand lift) does this campaign achieve? Gives a baseline understanding.

Multi-cell

Each strategy gets its own test and control groups. Answers: which strategy achieves more efficient incremental results? Audience A versus B, influencer versus brand creative, Feed versus Stories, frequency levels.

Nested

A test within a test: the parent’s test group is split again for a child test. Measures total investment and a subset at once. Use it as a last resort: the structure isn’t obvious in reporting, results are easier to misread, and setup is complex.

Study guide p. 32; i-DAC tutorial, measurement approach
What an A/B test does

Your audience is split into random, non-overlapping groups. Each group sees ad sets that are identical except for one variable; change more and you won’t know what caused the difference. The winner is the ad set with the lowest cost per result for the campaign objective, under your current attribution settings.

Variables you can test: audience, ad creative, placement (including Advantage+ placements), delivery optimization, budget structure and campaign objective.

No control group. An A/B test shows which version performs better, not whether either one caused incremental results. When the groups have different baselines, such as previous buyers versus new prospects, the comparison is hard to interpret.
Study guide pp. 23, 28, 35
When to use it
  • To determine best practices
  • For day-to-day strategic decisions
  • When you want results based on last-ad attribution
  • When the A and B groups have similar baselines
  • When you need a quick test that is easy to set up

Wind & Wool, a fashion retailer, tested a “Learn more” button against “Shop now”. Both linked to the same promotions page; the test showed which text drove results more efficiently.

Study guide p. 14; i-DAC tutorial, A/B testing
Setup checklist
  • Duration: at least 3 days, no more than 30 (guide). The i-DAC checklist suggests 1–2 conversion cycles or at least two weeks.
  • Budget: equal for both versions and high enough to exit the learning phase; a weekly ad set budget of at least 50× the one-day-click CPA, or 100× the average CPA to be safe.
  • Creative: a prominent call to action and branding throughout.
  • Audience: minimize overlap with other campaigns and ad accounts.
  • Dark period: consider a media-dark period before or after the test.
  • Data: the combined groups need at least 100 conversions before results appear. Wait until the end to judge.

How lift tests work
  1. RandomizeThe audience is split at random into test and control groups with similar characteristics.
  2. DeliverAds are delivered to the test group only. The control group is held out.
  3. Survey or recordBoth groups are polled (brand lift) or their conversions are recorded (conversion lift).
  4. CompareThe difference between the groups is the incremental impact of the campaign.

With statistically significant results, lift tests infer causality and measure incrementality, which proxy metrics such as clicks and likes can’t. Use them to measure incremental outcomes, to see how ads affect results, and to learn the cost per lift point so you can spend efficiently.

Study guide pp. 29–31
Brand lift survey-based
  • Each person in the test gets one poll question, chosen at random, 4–48 hours after ad delivery.
  • 7-day eligibility window: someone who doesn’t answer can be polled again 7 days later, possibly with a different question (only if the campaign runs longer than 7 days).
  • An ad recall question is required. Standard recall names the brand in the question; unaided recall puts it in the answers.
  • Needs at least 250 responses to one question, and a holdout.
  • Outputs: brand lift % overall and by demographic, cost per brand lift, test details, confidence, and benchmarks for your industry or region.
FunnelQuestion typeExample
UpperAd recall, awarenessHave you heard of [brand]?
MidFamiliarity, favorability, message associationHow familiar are you with [brand]?
LowerConsideration, action intent, recommendationHow likely are you to consider [brand]?

Setup: run 4–12 weeks, at about two impressions per person per week, with a minimum reach of 2 million and the market’s budget minimum (for example $30,000 in the US). Put the logo in the first 2–3 seconds, design for sound off, use square or vertical video, and write poll questions after you’ve seen the creative.

Study guide pp. 30, 36; i-DAC tutorial, brand lift
Conversion lift outcome-based
  • Records sales outcomes for both groups; the difference is the conversion lift.
  • Needs conversion data in Ads Manager from the Pixel, the SDK or the Conversions API (the API is recommended; higher Event Match Quality helps).
  • Uses intent to treat: everyone is analyzed in the group they were assigned to, which keeps the groups comparable.
  • Needs a holdout and at least 80% statistical power.
  • Outputs: conversion lift, cost per conversion lift, sales lift, ROAS lift, conversion lift %, breakdowns by demographic and attribution window, confidence.

It answers questions such as: should this campaign get more budget? How many conversions do my ads on this platform drive? How should I balance prospecting and retargeting? Which attribution model fits my business?

Setup: 1–2 conversion cycles or at least two weeks, enough budget to exit the learning phase, conversion events that reflect your primary goal, and no mid-test edits or early stops. Positive, significant results: continue or expand. Flat results: adjust and rerun.

Study guide p. 66
Will the test detect the effect?
  • Potential reach: a larger holdout means a larger control group to compare against, so more power. Increase reach or the control group to detect lift more easily.
  • Budget: budget doesn’t technically change power, but it sets the media weight. More spend means a bigger expected effect, and bigger effects are easier to detect.
  • Data coverage: can the tool tie orders from the app, website and stores back to impressions?
  • Time: does the duration fit best practice, such as 3–30 days for an A/B test?
  • Technology: can the platform run an RCT at all?
  • Validity: run power calculations, and consider a preliminary analysis such as a rudimentary marketing mix model.

The i-DAC course adds rules of thumb: hold out at least 20% of the audience, and budget to reach 50% or more of it.

Statistical outputs and validation metrics

Statistical outputs help you interpret the results of an analysis; validation metrics measure the quality of the analysis. Almost every output has a validation metric, and together they show how robust the data is. Sort each term into its group: select a term, then a group.

Study guide pp. 42–46
The least-squares line

Regression measures how a dependent variable changes when one or more independent variables change. The least-squares method finds the line that minimizes the sum of squared residuals, the gaps between actual and predicted values.

Y=a+bX

Y is the predicted value, a the intercept (the value of Y when X=0), b the slope (the change in Y for each 1-unit change in X) and X the independent variable.

Simple linear

One independent variable. How does ad spend affect sales?

Multiple linear

Two or more. How do ad spend, sales reps and price discounts affect sales?

Study guide pp. 47–48
Correlation and the sign of a coefficient

The correlation coefficient r runs from −1 to 1: near 1 is a strong positive relationship, near −1 a strong negative one, near 0 little or no linear relationship. Software calculates it for you.

In a regression, a positive coefficient means the dependent variable rises as the independent variable rises (an upward line); a negative one means it falls. The magnitude is the expected change per 1-unit increase.

Statistical significance tells you a relationship is unlikely to be chance. It doesn’t tell you the effect is big enough to matter to the business, so always look at the size of the effect too.

Conversion lift is the number of conversions that wouldn’t have happened without the ads. Confidence is how sure the tool is that the ads caused lift: it runs thousands of simulations of your test, and if lift appears in 80% of them, it is 80% confident. Results at 90% or higher are considered reliable.

SQL retrieves data from relational databases, where data lives in tables. Brigitte, an analyst at LaLueur, validates a campaign against the online store’s customers, sales and products tables.

SELECT first_name, last_name, age
FROM customers
WHERE city = 'New York' AND age > 35
ORDER BY age DESC
LIMIT 3
  • SELECT * returns every column; WHERE filters rows; AND adds conditions.
  • ORDER BY sorts ascending by default; add DESC for descending. LIMIT caps the rows returned.
  • Data types matter: numbers stored as TEXT break math operators. Common types are INTEGER, TEXT or VARCHAR, and BOOLEAN.

Reconcile results across solutions

Tools use different methodologies, so their numbers rarely match. Before you compare them, ask:

  • What data input is used, which events are included, and how is data collected across devices?
  • What time frame and what attribution window?
  • What methodology: observational (attribution, MMM) or a test (lift study)?
  • Does it use historical data, or data from similar campaigns or industries?

Examples: platforms define a video view or ThruPlay differently, and Ads Manager and Google Ads use different default attribution windows.

Avoid over-attribution

Over-attribution happens when ads get credit for conversions that would have happened even if people hadn’t seen them. Last-touch reports are prone to it, especially for audiences that already buy often. Run conversion lift tests regularly to measure the true impact of your ads.

Marketing mix model

A marketing mix model estimates how much each channel and non-marketing factor contributes to a KPI. Read Axion’s equation first, then use response curves to make a 10% budget cut cost as little as possible.

Axion’s model

Axion wants to know how sales units respond to price, TV rating points (TVRs) and impressions on Facebook and Instagram. Its marketing mix model, a regression on last year’s data, came out as:

Sales units=10,000−5,000×Price+500×TVRs+4,000×Impressionsmillions

Each coefficient is the change in sales for a 1-unit change in its variable, holding the others fixed: a $1 price rise costs 5,000 units, one more TVR adds 500, and a million more impressions add 4,000. The general form adds every factor that could move the KPI:

KPIt=β0+β1x1,t+β2x2,t+⋯+εt

Common traps
  • A/B tests choose a winner; they don’t measure incrementality.
  • Correlation isn’t causation: a reach-versus-ROAS scatter can’t prove reach drives ROAS.
  • Last touch over-credits audiences that would buy anyway, such as retargeting and previous customers.
  • Engagement isn’t success; tie metrics to business outcomes.
  • Don’t switch the primary KPI mid-campaign.
  • TV running alongside a randomized lift test reaches both groups equally, so it doesn’t bias the lift. Media outside the test that reaches the control group does contaminate it.
  • Budget raises the expected lift; reach and holdout size raise power.
  • Statistically significant doesn’t mean big enough to matter to the business.
  • SQL: one WHERE, conditions joined with AND; GROUP BY the column you want one row per value of.
